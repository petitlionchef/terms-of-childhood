Who Writes the Terms of Childhood?

Audio reflection · 7 min 37 sec

Read the Transcript

A few minutes ago, you agreed to the Terms and Conditions of Childhood.

You accepted them before you knew exactly what they contained. Refusing them would have meant that you could not continue.

As you moved through the agreement, you were given access to spaces, resources, and technologies. But that access remained limited and revocable. You were expected to use the Services responsibly. Your activity could be monitored, and violations could lead to consequences. You could request an exception, but an authorized adult might need to approve it. The Terms could also be revised, and your continued participation would count as acceptance.

Finally, you were invited to provide feedback, while final authority remained with the Providers.

The website borrows the structure and language of actual Terms of Use. Childhood is not literally a service, and children do not enter it by clicking a checkbox. That is precisely where the comparison becomes meaningful. Children enter families, schools, legal systems, and technological environments whose conditions already exist. They participate in these worlds without having written their original terms.

The central question of this project is therefore:

What kind of participation is possible when the conditions of participation have already been written by someone else?

Wendy Luttrell’s work provides my primary framework for approaching this question. She describes children as active interpreters and inventors of culture, rather than incomplete adults who simply receive the meanings adults give them. At the same time, she understands child-adult relationships as interdependent and relational, but structured by unequal power.

These ideas explain the tension built into the Terms. The user is not completely passive. The user reads, interprets, responds, follows some conditions, and may negotiate others. But this participation occurs within categories and procedures created by people and institutions with greater authority.

For me, agency does not mean unrestricted choice, complete independence, or the absence of rules. It is the situated capacity to interpret and act within relationships and conditions that one did not create alone.

A child might comply with a rule, question it, negotiate an exception, reinterpret its purpose, find a way around it, or use a resource differently from what adults intended. These actions are not all forms of resistance, and they do not all have the same consequences. But they show that children participate in the worlds that also govern them.

The website deliberately contrasts the forms of participation recognized by the Providers with forms of agency that exceed those procedures.

Near the end of the Terms, you were allowed to submit questions, preferences, information, and proposed changes. However, submitting feedback did not guarantee that a decision would change. The agreement also gave you no way to transform the Terms themselves. That absence is deliberate. The interface represents what the Providers are prepared to receive, not the full range of children’s agency.

This distinction connects with Haeny Yoon’s account of children’s civic participation. Children’s participation does not begin only when adults invite them into a formal process. It is already taking place through everyday conversations, play, relationships, disagreement, and collective action. Children may comply with, question, reinterpret, negotiate, reproduce, or seek to transform the conditions they inherit. But institutions do not recognize all of these actions, and not all of them produce visible change.

The unfinished request for an exception makes this boundary tangible. The Terms told you that you could request a review, but the request could not proceed without adult authorization. Such a procedure may make decisions more consistent. But it may also place another barrier between having a reason and having that reason recognized.

This does not mean that all rules or adult decisions are inherently oppressive. Clear rules can sometimes limit arbitrary judgment. Adults also have responsibilities that should not simply be transferred to children in the name of autonomy.

The more useful questions are how rules are created, how they are enforced, whether exceptions are genuinely accessible, and whether the people governed by them can influence their revision.

This is where bell hooks’s connection between theory and practice becomes important to me. Identifying that power exists is not the end of the analysis. Theory should help us make better judgments in practice. It should help us distinguish between a necessary boundary and an unnecessarily punitive system, between a meaningful procedure and a procedural barrier, and between allowing children to speak and allowing their participation to have consequences.

The Terms and Conditions of Childhood is not an argument for childhood without adults, rules, or responsibilities. It is an invitation to examine how those responsibilities are distributed and how authority is exercised.

Children do not write the original terms of childhood. But they are not merely governed by them either. They interpret and respond to the conditions they inherit, including in ways that institutions may not recognize.

The question left open by this agreement is not only who writes the Terms.

It is whether, when, and how children can participate in shaping the terms that govern their lives.
